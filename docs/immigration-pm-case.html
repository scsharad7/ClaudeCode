00

What changed from v0.1

v0.1 was written blind — the Drive folder wasn't reachable and the product definition in it was my inference. It was wrong. I had guessed at a case-file and filing-workflow product for high-skilled immigrants. The actual idea is a trusted peer community with an AI router — a different product, a different buyer, and a different set of risks.

This version is built from the six files in the folder. Everything below is drawn from those documents, tagged by which one, or is my own analysis, tagged as such. External figures carry a source link.

02

The recommendation

Build Landed's wedge, aim at ImmigrantConnect's vision, and use one name.

The two documents aren't competitors — they're the same venture at two altitudes, and each supplies what the other lacks. The deck supplies the destination and the institutional scaffolding: mission, market framing, brand values, legal and ethical architecture, ops model, partnership channels, funding story. That work is done, and it's good. The brief supplies the mechanism and the discipline: the density principle, a real beachhead, the agent pipeline, an explicit not-in-v1 list, and honest starting metrics.

Where they conflict, the brief wins on sequencing and the deck wins on destination. Concretely:

  1. Launch as Landed — Bay Area, Indian tech workers, one city. Not four states, not 100,000 registrations.
  2. Keep ImmigrantConnect's positioning line — real people, real answers, one trusted community for every step of the immigration journey deck. It's the best sentence in either document.
  3. Verified identity, pseudonymous asking. This resolves contradiction #4: verify every account, because the deck is right that trust is the product — but let people ask under a handle, because the brief is right that visa fear suppresses honest questions. Verification gates who may answer; anonymity protects who asks.
  4. Drop Toronto from Year 1. It buys a second regulatory regime and no additional density.
  5. Pick one name. My recommendation is Landed — short, ownable, trademarkable, and "You landed. Now belong." beats a category description. Honest counter-argument: ImmigrantConnect is the name Prathma has already presented and defended, and it's clearer to a resettlement-agency partner who has never heard of you. This one is yours and Prathma's to call — but make it once, this week, and don't carry two names forward.
03

The problem

"Picture landing in a new country with no one to ask: Is this rent fair? Is this visa notice real? Where do I find food that tastes like home? Today, that search happens in unmoderated Facebook groups and WhatsApp chats — full of good intentions, but no verification, no structure, and no accountability."

— ImmigrantConnect venture plan

The brief sharpens the same problem into ten questions the product must answer. They're worth keeping verbatim, because they are the product spec brief:

  • H-1B transfer filed 3 weeks ago, no receipt notice — normal, or panic?
  • On OPT STEM extension — can I freelance without violating status?
  • Which Santa Clara County DMV has the shortest wait?
  • Where do I find curry leaves and methi in Sunnyvale that aren't $8 a bunch?
  • Weekend badminton group in the South Bay that welcomes newcomers?
  • First US paycheck — credit card, 401k, or pay off the card?
  • Risky to switch jobs 8 months into an H-1B?
  • Landlord wants 3 months' deposit for no credit history — legal in California?
  • Indian young professionals in Seattle who aren't coworkers?
  • Employer mentioned layoffs — what happens to my H-1B, and how many days do I have?
Analysis

What makes this list strong is that it spans two categories with completely different failure modes. Where do I find curry leaves is cheap to get wrong. How many days do I have after a layoff can end someone's status. A product that treats those identically is either useless or dangerous. The routing decision in §6 is the whole product, and this list is why.

04

Why now

The deck's case is demographic; the brief's is technological. Both are real — and there's a third neither document uses: the system got measurably worse in the last 18 months, which raises the value of every "is this normal?" answer. analysis

51.9MForeign-born US residents, mid-2025 deck
11.3MCases pending at USCIS, end FY2026 Q1 — up 17% YoY
86Cases completed per 100 received — 11th straight quarter below break-even
211,600FY2027 H-1B registrations for 85,000 slots, newly wage-weighted

Demographics deck — 51.9M foreign-born residents; 93M people in immigrant-oriented households; ~70% concentrated in CA, TX, FL and NY. Sources: Pew Research Center (2025); Migration Policy Institute (2025).

Technology brief — the classifier-router pattern is now reliable enough that an app can decide, per question, whether to answer or fetch a human. That decision is what separates Landed from a chatbot.

System stress analysis — the net backlog inside the government's own control went from under 4.3M to 6.3M in twelve months (AIC). The $100,000 H-1B fee was signed in September 2025 and struck down in June 2026 — a year of career decisions made against a rule that no longer exists. Every one of those is a reason Priya asks Rahul "is this normal?", and none existed in this form when the last generation of immigrant community apps launched.

Analysis

Rahul is the whole business. Priya's problem is solved the moment Rahul exists nearby; Rahul's motivation is reciprocity, not money, and reciprocity is fragile — it survives about three unanswered notifications. Treat supply-side retention as a primary metric, not a secondary one. The deck's "trained category leads" is the same instinct expressed as an org chart; the honest version is that you will personally recruit the first 50 Rahuls, and they will stay because of you, not the product.

06

What we build

v1 scope brief — unchanged, it's right

In: AI Q&A for visa, daily life and local questions · profile (country, visa type, city, years in US, ≤5 interests) · human matching ("3 people near you answered this recently") · pseudonymous asking · basic city feed, filterable by category.

Out, and say it out loud: no events, no DMs or group chat, no second country, no mobile app until 1,000 users, no monetization until you know what people would pay for.

The agent pipeline brief

#AgentJob
1ClassifierQuestion → {category, risk, confidence, route}. Risk high = wrong advice could harm status, legal standing or finances.
2RouterPure orchestration, no LLM call of its own. ai → answer only · human → matching only · both → fast context plus a notified human.
3AnswerGenerates the factual response where the AI can be relied on.
4SafetyAttaches disclaimers to anything legal-adjacent.
5MatchingScores helpers by same visa type, same city, recency of the same experience, track record — then notifies the best one.

Plus a feedback loop logging whether the answer helped and whether the human replied, which tunes routing over time.

Analysis

The strongest design decision in the whole folder is storing the classifier's reasoning — category, risk, confidence, route — on the question row, not just the text. That turns the agent's judgment into an auditable dataset, and it's the only thing that will let you answer, six months in, "which categories should we stop letting the AI answer?" That dataset is also the closest thing this product has to a durable moat.

Stack brief

Next.js 14 + TypeScript on Vercel → FastAPI on Railway → Supabase (Postgres + pgvector + auth) → Claude API. Langfuse for tracing, Resend for email. Seven tables, UUID keys, created_at everywhere; AI answers and human answers deliberately kept in separate tables because they have different shapes and lifecycles.

07

Market opportunity

Analysis

51.9M is a population, not a market. It's the right number for a mission statement and the wrong one for a revenue model — it implies nothing about willingness to pay, and community products convert at a fraction of a percent, not at SaaS rates. The number that decides this business is the deck's own $52–$58 contribution margin per subscriber — quoted without a CAC beside it. If blended CAC exceeds $52, the model inverts no matter how large the population is.

Bottom-up, for the wedge actually being built analysis

LayerBasisEstimate
Indian-born immigrants in the USMPI / Pew order of magnitude~2.9M
Working-age professionals + studentsShare of the above~1.2M
Bay Area Indian-origin tech workersBeachhead~150k–250k
Density threshold for the product to work briefActive users in one metro500

The first target isn't a market size — it's 500 people in one metro. Everything above that line is Year 2+ and should be treated as unearned until the matching loop demonstrably works.

Revenue-relevant SAM, once the loop works: if the wedge generalises to ~1.2M working-age Indian immigrants nationally, and a community product converts 2–4% to a $60–96/yr tier, that's $1.4M–$4.6M of in-segment subscription revenue. Reaching the deck's Year 5 numbers therefore requires the expansion to all-immigrant, all-metro, plus B2B. That expansion is an assumption, not a projection — say so before someone else does.

Business model — and the model that disagrees with itself

Two-sided freemium deck. Demand: new arrivals and students. Supply: established immigrants and verified professionals. Revenue: Connect+ subscriptions, directory listings, B2B partnerships, verified-professional subscriptions. Partners: immigration law firms, resettlement nonprofits, universities, fintechs.

The numbers don't reconcile — fix this first analysis

The deck carries two financial models, built in different weeks and never merged. The roadmap slide names both out loud ("$21.0M net revenue (Wk3 model) / $10.5M (Wk7 model)"), so this is known — but it's currently sitting inside a presented deck.

Business-model slide (Wk3)Financial-plan slide (Wk7)Gap
Year 1 net revenue$1.2M$310K3.9×
Year 5 net revenue$21M$10.52M2.0×
Total funding$17M (Seed + A + B)$2.0M (SAFE + A + SBA)8.5×
Year 3EBITDA break-evenProfitable, $180K netconsistent
Revenue streamsConnect+, listings, B2B, pro subsPremium subs, listings, sponsorships, consultation feesnamed differently

Recommendation: keep the Wk7 model — $2.0M raised, $310K Year 1, $10.5M Year 5. It's the more defensible of the two, its funding ask matches a pre-PMF community product, and a $17M raise is not credible against 100 active users. Rebuild the business-model slide from it and retire the old figures everywhere they appear.

Two more model problems analysis

  • "Service remains free" versus subscription-led revenue. Strategic objective 5 commits to the service staying free; the revenue model is led by subscriptions. Both can be true — free core, paid Connect+ — but the deck never says which parts are which. Write the line: what is free forever, and what is paid.
  • "Consultation fees" and law-firm partnerships need a lawyer before they need a spreadsheet. Revenue arrangements between a non-lawyer platform and immigration attorneys run into state bar rules on fee-splitting with non-lawyers and paying for referrals (ABA Model Rules 5.4 and 7.2, as adopted state by state). Flat-fee directory listings are the conventional structure; per-referral or revenue-share fees are the structure that gets platforms in trouble. Get an opinion before this line item goes in front of an investor. This flags a risk; it isn't a legal conclusion.
09

Competition — and the ghost in the room

Combined from both documents: Facebook and WhatsApp groups (no verification, no structure) · Nextdoor (hyperlocal, no immigrant categories) · USAHello and Settle In (resources, no peer channel) · Meetup (events, no visa matching) · FindHello (directory) · Boundless (visa legal) · Tarjimly (translation) · Google and Reddit (generic).

Homeis — the most important competitor is the one that's already dead analysis

The deck lists Homeis as a live competitor with "limited language support, narrow ethnic focus." The more useful fact: Homeis shut down in July 2021, after raising a $12M Series A from Canaan Partners and Spark Capital for precisely this product — a language- and location-based immigrant community network. It grew during COVID, then failed to hold retention or find monetization. The domain went up for sale. (Startup Nation Central; summary)

This turns the competitive slide from a list into a thesis. A well-funded, well-staffed version of this idea has already been tried, and it died on the two exact risks named in your own SWOT: retention and monetization. Any version of this plan that doesn't answer "why doesn't this end like Homeis?" is incomplete.

The answer, and it's a good one

Homeis was a social network. Landed is a question-answering utility with a social supply side. Social networks need constant novelty to retain. A utility needs only to be there when the question arrives — retention is measured in questions answered, not daily sessions. That distinction deserves a slide of its own, and it's why the brief's "no events, no DMs, no feed-scrolling" discipline is a strategic choice rather than a scoping shortcut.

10

Trust, legal and ethics

deck GDPR/CCPA-aligned multilingual privacy policy · layered IP · ToS/EULA and vendor DPAs · governance board including immigrant-advocacy expertise · free core access · fair labour and wellbeing protections for moderators · independent audits · ethical review board with a member reporting channel. Hybrid AI and human moderation, supported by research that AI moderation is trusted comparably to human moderation when paired with transparency and appeal (Molina & Sundar, 2022).

brief Encryption at rest and in transit · never train on user data, and say so plainly · one-click delete-all · user-scoped storage, no cross-user leakage by design · report and block before launch.

Three additions analysis

  1. Say what you do when the government asks. Both documents cover privacy from a GDPR/CCPA angle — compliance with commercial regulation. Neither addresses the fear this population actually has, which is not marketing spam. Publish a plain-language policy on how you respond to government data requests, and minimise what you hold so the answer stays short. This is a feature, and it's the strongest trust differentiator available to you.
  2. The safety agent is a legal control, not a UX nicety. Routing high-risk visa questions to humans with disclaimers is what keeps the platform on the right side of unauthorised-practice-of-law lines. Treat a mis-route on a risk: high question as a Sev-1, with a written escalation path.
  3. Moderator wellbeing is already in the deck — keep it. It's unusual, it's correct, and it signals a team that has thought past the demo.

Metrics

The brief's targets are right for Phases 1–2: 50+ landing-page signups before building, 100 active users in 30 days, 40% asking a second question within a week. What's missing is the supply side and safety analysis. Together, the v1 scorecard:

LayerMetricTarget
DemandSecond question within 7 days brief40%
SupplyHelper reply rate within 24h≥60%
SupplyHelpers answering ≥2 questions in 30 days≥40%
Match qualityAsker marks the match helpful≥70%
RoutingHigh-risk questions correctly routed to a human100%
RoutingAI-answered questions later escalated by the asker<10%
DensityActive users in launch metro500
CostBlended cost per answered questiontrack from day 1

North Star: questions answered well, per active user, per month — where "well" means the asker marked it helpful and no escalation followed. It moves only if routing, supply and demand are all working, which is exactly the property you want from a single number.

Open decisions — for Sharad & Prathma

Ordered by how much each unblocks.

  1. One name. Landed or ImmigrantConnect. Recommendation in §2 — decide it this week and retire the other everywhere.
  2. One financial model. Recommendation: the Wk7 model. Rebuild the business-model slide from it. (§8)
  3. Whose venture is this? A capstone, a startup, or a portfolio project? The deck asks for a seed round and a founding team; the brief is a solo nine-weekend build. Both are legitimate; they are not the same commitment, and the answer changes everything from Phase 2 on.
  4. Beachhead: Indian tech workers, or all immigrants? The density argument is strong and I'd take it — but it narrows the mission the deck is built on, and Prathma should agree to that narrowing explicitly rather than discover it later.
  5. Toronto: in or out of Year 1? Recommendation: out. (§1)
  6. What's free forever? Write the line before the first paying user, not after. (§8)
  7. Legal opinion on the law-firm revenue streams before they appear in an investor deck. (§8)
