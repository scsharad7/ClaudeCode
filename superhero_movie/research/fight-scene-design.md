# Fight Scene Design: A Practical Guide for a First Superhero Feature

**Budget tiers used throughout:**
- **Tier 1, micro:** under ~$50K total. Friends, a borrowed location, one or two paid stunt days.
- **Tier 2, low:** ~$50K–$300K. A small paid stunt team for the action days, light VFX.
- **Tier 3, modest indie:** ~$300K–$2M+. A full stunt department, rigging, a second unit, a real VFX vendor.

---

## 1. What Makes a Fight Scene Intense

A fight is intense when the audience understands what is at stake, can follow it, and can't guess how it ends. Choreography skill comes after that.

Almost everything below is free: it's writing and planning. Only escalation (shoot days), breakaway props, and practical stunts cost real money.

| Principle | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Stakes inside the fight** | Each fighter wants something specific beyond "win": reach the detonator, protect the kid, get the drive. |
| **Every fight changes something** | Afterward a relationship, power, injury, or plot point is different. If cutting the fight loses nothing, cut it. |
| **Clear geography** | Establish the space early (wide shot, door, ledge, objective). The audience always knows where the exit, weapon, and threat are. |
| **Escalation and reversals** | The advantage flips every 10–20 seconds: new weapon, new opponent, injury, trick, location change. |
| **Character-specific style** | A brawler fights dirty, a trained hero efficiently, a showoff villain plays with his food. |
| **Lose before you win** | The hero takes real damage that carries into later scenes. Invulnerable heroes kill tension. |
| **Use the environment** | Doors, railings, furniture, and stairs become weapons, obstacles, and turning points. |
| **Breathing beats** | Short pauses to reassess, bleed, or trade a line. Fights need rhythm like music. |
| **Sound** | Impact layers, breath, cloth, body falls. Up to half of a "hit" is what you hear, and it's the cheapest upgrade available. |
| **Practical impact** | Real bodies hitting real surfaces, with CG adding to that rather than replacing it. |

### Exciting vs. weightless CG mush

- **What excites audiences:** visible proof the performer is doing it (wide frames, long takes), hits with consequences, a physically present camera, cause and effect. Stahelski's rule on *John Wick* was no shaky cam and no cutting away ([Slashfilm](https://slashfilm.com/938223/the-director-had-to-fight-for-the-long-takes-in-john-wick-comic-con)).
- **What reads as mush:** massless digital bodies, camera moves no operator could make, rapid cutting that hides weak choreography, unclear space, and nobody getting hurt. When everything can fly and nothing hurts, nothing matters.
- **Rule of thumb:** shoot a real performer making a real contact beat, then let VFX add or clean up (rig removal, energy blasts, debris). *Fury Road* used CG mostly to erase rigs and cables ([Adorama](https://www.adorama.com/alc/behind-the-scenes-of-mad-max-fury-road-stunt-work)).

## 2. Case Studies: What to Steal

**The Raid (2011)**. Dir. Gareth Evans; choreography by Iko Uwais and Yayan Ruhian.
Evans spent about three months designing the fights with only Uwais, Ruhian, crash mats, and a Handycam. He gave them the opponent count, weapons, location, and tone, and they filled in every move. He budgets **2–3 shoot days per minute of nonstop fighting** ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=131257)). **Steal:** a single location (one building) and the shoot-days-per-minute math.

**John Wick (2014)**. Dir. Chad Stahelski (co-founder of [87Eleven](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/87eleven-stunts) with David Leitch).
Long, wide, choreographed takes that show Keanu Reeves really doing it, shot mostly in camera. Stahelski stresses that the DP has to see the rehearsed fight before planning the wide shots ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wick_(film)), [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2019/05/john-wick-chapter-3-parabellum-director-on-the-films-epic-stunts-real-dogs/)). **Steal:** train your lead for months, frame wide, and let a consistent fighting system (gun-fu) define the character.

**Captain America: The Winter Soldier (2014), elevator and highway**. Dirs. Russo brothers; stunt coordinator Thomas Robinson Harper; fight choreographer James Young; second unit Spiro Razatos.
Harper made "videovis" (filmed rehearsals) of every action scene for the Russos ([Flickering Myth](https://www.flickeringmyth.com/2014/05/fighting-patriot-thomas-robinson-harper-talks-captain-america-winter-soldier)). The elevator fight was rehearsed in a mock-up elevator with about a dozen stunt performers ([Men's Health AU](https://menshealth.com.au/chris-evans-fight-training-elevator-scene-winter-soldier/)), and its dread builds before the first punch. The highway introduces the Winter Soldier as an unstoppable force, moving from car to street to hand-to-hand. **Steal:** a confined space with too many enemies, tension before the first punch, and a villain introduced by what he can do.

**Daredevil S1E2, hallway fight (2015)**. Stunt and fight coordinator Philip Silvera (who also appears in the Winter Soldier elevator fight).
The fight looks like one continuous take down a hallway and was designed in about a day and a half ([Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/537179/daredevil-fight-scene/), [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=673192)). Matt is exhausted, sloppy, and gets beaten up, and the audience feels every second. **Steal:** a TV-budget showstopper made from one corridor, one camera move, and exhaustion as the drama.

**Spider-Man 2 (2004), train fight**. Dir. Sam Raimi; second-unit director Dan Bradley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Bradley)).
It escalates from a building wall to the top of a train to inside it. The stakes shift from beating Doc Ock to saving the passengers, which costs Peter his mask and nearly his life. The passengers carrying him afterward gives the fight its emotional payoff ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/why-spider-man-2s-train-fight-is-superhero-cinemas-greatest-action-scene/)). **Steal:** the hero gives up the fight to save people. The win is a sacrifice.

**Shang-Chi (2021), bus fight**. Supervising stunt coordinator Brad Allan; fight coordinator Andy Cheng (both Jackie Chan Stunt Team alumni).
A Jackie Chan-style homage with props, comedy, a moving vehicle, and nine tracked civilians ([fxguide](https://www.fxguide.com/?p=282622), [MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2021/11/shang-chi-and-the-legend-of-the-ten-rings-stunt-coordinator-andy-cheng-on-that-epic-bus-fight/)). VFX blended takes and replaced faces, but the stunts stayed real ([VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/mastering-the-martial-arts/)). **Steal:** the reveal that the hero has hidden skills, the environment as a toolbox, and humor inside the danger.

**Logan (2017)**. Dir. James Mangold; stunt coordinator / second unit Garrett Warren ([IMDb](https://m.imdb.com/name/nm0912836)).
Brutal, R-rated violence, and a hero who is visibly failing: his healing is slow and he limps. Laura's fighting style (feral, acrobatic) contrasts with Logan's (exhausted, blunt) ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2017/06/watch-how-vfx-created-wolverines-claws-logan)). **Steal:** the power has a cost and is fading, and two fighters with contrasting styles share a scene.

**Nobody (2021), bus fight**. Dir. Ilya Naishuller; stunt coordinator Greg Rementer; 87North.
It was the first action scene shot, with three nights scheduled, split into three sections rather than faked as a single take. Odenkirk gets hurt as badly as he hurts anyone ([Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/nobody-director-interview), [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/nobody-bus-fight)). **Steal:** a rusty hero, ugly and clumsy fighting, everyday props (seats, pull cord), and the one fight that tells the audience who this man is.

**Mad Max: Fury Road (2015)**. Dir. George Miller; supervising stunt coordinator Guy Norris.
About 150 stunt performers. Miller has said about 80% of the film is practical, with CG mostly used for rig removal ([Adorama](https://www.adorama.com/alc/behind-the-scenes-of-mad-max-fury-road-stunt-work), [CinemaBlend](https://www.cinemablend.com/news/636949/One-Mad-Max-Fury-Road-Stunt-Made-Producer-Think-Twice)). The framing is center-weighted, so fast cuts stay readable. **Steal:** keep the subject in the center of frame so a quick edit still reads, and use real physics.

**Atomic Blonde (2017), stairwell**. Dir. David Leitch (87Eleven).
A ~10-minute "single take" stitched from many hand-operated takes via whip-pans and body wipes, shot chronologically so injury makeup could build ([Slashfilm](https://slashfilm.com/964117/how-atomic-blondes-amazing-stairwell-fight-came-together), [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-features/atomic-blonde-director-how-i-made-five-kick-ass-action-sequences-194703/)). Both fighters are exhausted and keep getting up. **Steal:** a fake oner with hidden stitches is achievable on an indie budget, and the fighters' exhaustion becomes the spectacle.

Also study **Sam Hargrave**: the former Captain America double and coordinator directed *Extraction*'s ~12-minute stitched oner while strapped to a vehicle holding the camera ([MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2020/04/stunt-coordinator-turned-director-sam-hargrave-on-his-action-packed-debut-extraction/), [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/netflix-extraction-one-take-action-sequence/)). Stunt people make excellent action directors.

## 3. The Professional Fight Pipeline

| Step | What happens | Who owns it | Micro-budget version |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Fight design** | Story beats become a beat list: wants, reversals, signature move, ending. | Director + fight choreographer | You write beats; a hired choreographer turns them into moves. |
| **2. Stuntvis / previs** | Stunt team performs the fight on gym mats, films it from planned angles, and **cuts it** (often on a phone or laptop, same day). This is Harper's "videovis"; the high end is Guy and Harrison Norris's real-time stuntvis ([fxguide](https://www.fxguide.com/?p=244841)). | Stunt coordinator, choreographer, performers | **Essential and nearly free:** one gym day, phones, CapCut or Resolve. See Eric Jacobus's [Die Hard previz](https://ericjacobus.com/2013/03/27/the-deleted-fight-from-a-good-day-to-die-hard-previz-by-eric-jacobus-alvin-hsing-and-j-j-perry/). |
| **3. Approval** | Director, DP, producer review and revise. The cut becomes the shot list. | Director | Same |
| **4. Rehearsal** | Actors learn choreography over weeks; safety, timing, and selling hits get drilled. | Choreographer, coordinator | 2–5+ sessions for leads; doubles for hard moves. |
| **5. Camera planning** | DP copies stuntvis angles; picks lenses, rigs, shutter, lighting that allows wides. | DP + coordinator | Copy stuntvis angles exactly. |
| **6. Shoot** | In sections, safety first; the coordinator can stop any take. | 1st AD runs set; coordinator owns stunt safety | Book separate "action days." |
| **7. Edit** | Rhythm and impact, not chaos. | Editor, with coordinator input | Stuntvis cut is the template. |
| **8. Sound** | Impact layers, Foley, breath, silence before big hits. | Sound designer, Foley | DIY Foley plus a ~$50–$200 library. |
| **9. VFX** | Rig removal, blood, powers, face replacement, stitches. | VFX supervisor/vendor | Cleanup and power layers only. |

### Coverage strategies

- **Long takes / oners:** the audience trusts what it can see is real. Needs heavy rehearsal and capable performers, and one mistake costs the take. Stitched oners (Atomic Blonde, Extraction) reduce that risk.
- **Cut-heavy:** works only if every shot is a complete action ("hit lands," "body lands") and the subject stays center-frame (Fury Road). It fails when cuts hide choreography that isn't there.
- **Recommended hybrid:** wide masters of each 5–15 move "phrase," plus inserts (impact, face, hand reaching for the weapon). **Wide shows the skill, tight shows the pain.**
- **Lenses:** wide (18–35mm) close to the action keeps geography and a physical camera. Long lenses compress distance and help **cheat hits**: a 6–12 inch off-axis miss reads as contact.
- **Shutter:** 180° gives natural blur. A narrow shutter (45–90°) gives a staccato, hard-edged look (Saving Private Ryan, Gladiator) for brutal moments. Test it, because it can strobe.
- **Frame rate:** slight undercranking (22–23fps) adds speed. Overcrank (48–120fps) only for signature beats.

### Edit rules

- **Don't cut too fast.** Let the hit land and the reaction register, usually ~4–12 frames past contact.
- **Impact frames:** cut on or one frame after contact. A 1–3 frame hold, flash frame, or micro-shake adds punch.
- **Overlap the cut:** repeating 2–4 frames of a big hit across an angle change (Hong Kong style) sells force.
- **Rhythm:** phrases separated by breathers. A fight at one constant speed feels slow.

## 4. Safety and Legal Essentials (Non-Negotiable)

These rules apply at every budget tier.

1. **Hire a qualified stunt coordinator** for any fights, falls, weapons, fire, vehicles, or wires. Their call is final on safety. On SAG-AFTRA low-budget agreements, **a stunt coordinator is required if there are stunts**, paid at Basic Agreement rates ([SAG-AFTRA one-sheet](https://sagaftra.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/Low%20Budget%20Entertainment%20Contracts%20One%20Sheet.pdf)), roughly **$1,246/day** as of mid-2025 per SAG-AFTRA's low-budget rate materials ([SAG-AFTRA](https://www.sagaftra.org/files/LowBudgetTheatricalAgreement.pdf)). Verify current rates.
2. **SAG-AFTRA micro-budget agreement (≤$20K) does not permit stunts.** If your film is union and has fights, plan on at least the Ultra Low Budget agreement (~$300K cap) or another tier that allows stunts. The Short Project and Student agreements allow stunts under conditions ([SAG-AFTRA](https://sagaftra.org/sites/default/files/sa_documents/low_budget_entertainment_contracts_one_sheet.pdf)).
3. **Rehearse.** Never debut choreography on the day. No improvised stunts.
4. **Protection:** crash mats, hidden pads, soft or rubber prop weapons, breakaway glass and furniture, mouthguards where unseen.
5. **Wires:** qualified riggers only, rated hardware, a written rigging plan, a sandbag/stunt-performer test before the actor, a dedicated safety on every line. Never DIY.
6. **Weapons:** a licensed armorer, never live ammunition. Use rubber/airsoft props and CG muzzle flashes. Follow the industry [safety bulletins](https://www.csatf.org/production-safety/safety-bulletins/).
7. **Insurance:** general liability that **declares the stunts**. Insurers often want the coordinator's résumé and a stunt breakdown; undeclared stunts can void coverage.
8. **On set:** a medic every action day, a safety meeting before each stunt, clear "cut" authority, and a take limit on big impacts (fatigue causes injuries).
9. **Locations:** permits, and notify police if a fight is visible from the street or uses prop weapons.

## 5. Superhero-Specific Design

### Sell powers on a budget

| Power | Cheap trick | Upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| **Super strength** | Foam/breakaway props, hidden ratchet pull for "thrown" reactions, low angles, deep bass hit | Air rams, CG debris |
| **Speed** | Undercranked or ramped footage, dropped frames, whoosh plus wind on hair and clothes, post blur | CG trails |
| **Flight / leaps** | Cut on takeoff, land on landing (apple-box jump, drop into frame), mini-tramp, low angle against sky | Wires (pro riggers only), crane |
| **Energy blasts** | Real light on set (gelled LED or flash at impact), smoke, 2D comp | Full CG FX |
| **Telekinesis** | Fishing line or rods painted out, objects run in reverse | Wire-pulled props |
| **Durability** | The hero barely reacts while the attacker's hand breaks | Breakaway weapons, prosthetics |

The cheapest power effect of all is **the other person's reaction plus sound design**.

### Power set rules (where the tension comes from)

- **Clear rules:** what it does, range, duration. Show them early.
- **A cost:** pain, exhaustion, cooldown, collateral damage, a moral price. Logan's slowed healing is the model.
- **A weakness** the villain can exploit and the hero overcomes by growing, not by a new power appearing.
- **Mastery arc:** clumsy in Act 1, creative in Act 3.
- **Sanderson's rule:** limitations are more interesting than powers. Show what the hero *can't* do.

### Hero vs. villain style contrast

Design the styles as opposites so every exchange tells a story: defensive vs. aggressive, scrappy vs. precise, close vs. long range, protects bystanders vs. uses them. Make the villain a dark mirror of what the hero could become.

## 6. Three-Act Fight Escalation Template

| | **Act 1: Opening fight** | **Act 2: Midpoint fight** | **Act 3: Climax fight** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Establish the hero's limits, style, and power rules. Hook the audience. | Raise the stakes. The hero **loses**, and that loss costs something. | Resolve the theme. The hero wins **through growth** (a new understanding, choice, or sacrifice), not just strength. |
| **Emotional beat** | Excitement and promise ("this person is special but raw") | Shock, humiliation, grief | Catharsis, earned triumph |
| **Fight outcome** | A scrappy win or a draw against weaker enemies. Shows the gap between the hero and the real threat. | A defeat against the villain or lieutenant. Injury, a loss of trust, an ally hurt, a secret exposed. | A reversal of the midpoint: the hero beats the move that beat them before. |
| **Scale** | Small: 1–4 opponents, one location, 60–120 sec of fighting | Medium: the villain shows full power, 2–3 minutes, collateral damage | The largest: multiple phases, possibly a location change, 3–6 min |
| **Set-piece ideas** | Alley mugging interrupted, a convenience store robbery, a subway car, a bar | The villain ambushes the hero's home or workplace, a parking garage, a rooftop where the hero is thrown off | A stairwell or tower climb (escalating floors), a moving vehicle, a collapsing building, the villain's lair turned against them |
| **Budget lever** | 1 night, 1 location, 2–4 stunt players | 2–3 days, breakaways, 1–2 rigs | 3–6 days, the largest share of stunt and VFX budget. Stitched-oner potential. |

**Callback trick:** plant a move or weakness in Act 1, have the villain exploit it in Act 2, then let the hero turn it into the winning move in Act 3.

## 8. Micro-Budget Playbook

1. **Hire the stunt team for action days only.** One coordinator plus 2–4 performers for 2–6 days stretches money furthest. Local stunt teams and martial-arts studios often have reel-ready performers; search "stunt team reel [your city]."
2. **Film the stuntvis yourself.** A gym or dojo for a day, phones at your planned angles, cut the same night. That gives you a shot list, a schedule, and an investor pitch.
3. **Cast fighters, or train actors early.** A martial artist who can act beats any effect (Evans built *The Raid* around Uwais). Otherwise give your lead 6–12 weeks of training and double the flips and falls.
4. **One great location beats five mediocre ones.** Stairwells, hallways, garages, warehouses, and rooftops (with pro fall protection). The Raid, Daredevil, Atomic Blonde, and Nobody all use confined spaces, which give clear geography, cheap lighting, and natural reversals.
5. **Practical over CG.** Sugar-glass and balsa breakaways, air or bladder blood rigs run by the coordinator (no pyro), debris thrown off-camera, hidden pull rigs. CG only for rig removal and power glows.
6. **Put money into sound.** A strong Foley and impact pass makes cheap footage feel expensive, and celery, cabbage, leather, and gym-mat body falls cost almost nothing.
7. **Plan one showcase fight** with the most days, possibly a stitched oner. Keep the others short and sharp.
8. **Shoot fights early** (as Nobody did): fresh performers, and the result sets the tone and the trailer.
9. **Study the free reference material:**
   - [Corridor Crew](https://www.youtube.com/@CorridorCrew), especially their *Stuntmen React* series with guests like Andy Cheng, Amy Johnston, and Luke LaFontaine ([episode list](https://thetvdb.com/series/the-corridor-crew/episodes/7545954)), and their VFX breakdowns.
   - Stunt team reels: 87Eleven/87North, Jackie Chan Stunt Team, The Stunt People (Eric Jacobus), and the YouTube action shorts of Jacobus and others.
   - Eric Jacobus's book *Badass on a Budget*, written for zero-budget action filmmakers ([ComicMix](https://comicmix.com/2026/05/09/bad-ass-on-a-budget-is-for-indie-filmmakers/)).
   - Behind-the-scenes rehearsal footage, such as the Winter Soldier elevator rehearsal and the Nobody bus blocking ([Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/581759/nobody-clip-exclusive/)).

### Budget scaling snapshot

| Item | Micro | Low | Modest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stunt coordinator | 2–4 days (design + key shoot days) | Full action days + prep | Full prep + shoot, plus a second unit |
| Stunt performers | 2–4, fight days only | 4–8 | 10+ plus doubles |
| Stuntvis | Phones, a gym, one day | Multiple days, a proper edit | Multi-camera stuntvis, previs vendor |
| Rigs | Ratchet/pull rigs via the coordinator, no flying | Basic wires with pro riggers | Full wire/descender work, air rams |
| VFX | Cleanup + simple 2D power glows | Wire removal, comps, blood | Digital doubles, full power FX |
| Sound | DIY Foley + libraries | A hired sound designer | Full Foley stage + mix |

**Bottom line:** story stakes, clear geography, real bodies, rehearsal, and great sound cost little and matter most. Spend money on stunt professionals and safety first, and VFX last.
