# Story Development: From Rough Idea to Shooting Script

*A practical guide for a first-time filmmaker writing an original superhero feature where the fights carry the story.*

**The one rule that runs through this whole guide:** a fight scene is only as good as the question it answers. Before every fight, the audience should know what the hero wants, what they stand to lose, and what their power *can't* do. Everything below is a way to set those three things up.

---

## 1. The Development Ladder

Climb it one rung at a time. Each document is cheap to fix and expensive to skip. The time estimates are rough figures for a first-timer working part-time (about 10 hours a week).

| Step | What it is | Typical length | First-timer time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Idea** | The premise, plus *why you* should be the one to tell it | 1–3 sentences | Days to weeks |
| **Logline** | Hero + flaw, inciting incident, goal, antagonist, stakes | 1 sentence (25–50 words) | 1–2 weeks (write 20 versions) |
| **One-page synopsis** | The whole story in prose: beginning, middle, end, with the ending included | 1 page | 1 week |
| **Beat sheet** | 15–40 numbered story beats, each 1–2 lines (see §2) | 2–4 pages | 2–3 weeks |
| **Treatment** | Present-tense prose version of the film with key moments, tone and set pieces | 5–20 pages (10 is common) | 3–5 weeks |
| **Outline / scene list** | Every scene as a numbered slugline plus what changes in it. Your fights are budgeted here | 40–70 scenes, 5–15 pages | 3–4 weeks |
| **First draft** | Full screenplay in industry format. Finish it before you polish anything | 90–110 pages | 2–4 months |
| **Rewrites** | Structural pass, then character, dialogue and finally a budget/production pass | Draft 2 to Draft 6+ | 3–9 months |
| **Locked shooting script** | Scene numbers locked. Later changes go on colored revision pages | Same length | Weeks, in step with prep |

**Realistic total:** 9–18 months from idea to a shootable script. Most first-timers lose time by skipping the beat sheet and outline, then rewriting a 100-page draft three times to fix structural problems a 3-page document would have exposed.

## 2. Structure Applied to Superhero Films

### 2a. Three-act structure: *Spider-Man* (2002)

| Act | Pages/minutes (of ~120) | What happens | Major fights |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Act 1, Setup** | 0–30 | Peter is bitten, discovers his powers and uses them selfishly. Norman becomes Goblin | Wrestling match vs. Bone Saw (powers used for money, no stakes) |
| **Act 1 turn** | ~35 | Uncle Ben is killed by the thief Peter let go | Chase of the carjacker. **The first fight with real stakes, driven by guilt** |
| **Act 2, Confrontation** | 30–90 | Spider-Man rises, the Goblin escalates, and Peter's two lives collide | World Unity Festival attack (MJ in danger), burning building, Goblin attacks Aunt May |
| **Act 3, Resolution** | 90–120 | Goblin learns his identity and forces a choice | Queensboro Bridge: save MJ **or** the tram (the power can't do both, so the choice is the fight). Final brawl, then Peter gives up MJ |

**Lesson:** every fight raises *personal* stakes, moving from Peter's money to his uncle, then strangers, his family and finally his love. The final fight is a moral choice dressed up as action.

### 2b. Save the Cat beat sheet: *Iron Man* (2008)

Blake Snyder's 15 beats are placed against a ~110-page script ([Nelson ThoughtHub](https://www.nelson.edu/thoughthub/the-beat-sheet-skeleton-of-a-screenplay); [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)). Snyder himself stressed they are "skeletons, not absolute formulas."

| Beat (target page) | *Iron Man* | Fight? |
|---|---|---|
| Opening Image (1) | Tony joking in a Humvee in Afghanistan: arrogant, untouchable | |
| Theme Stated (5) | Tony's cynicism about the arms trade. Yinsen later says it outright: "a man who has everything and nothing" | |
| Set-Up (1–10) | Flashback: genius, playboy, weapons dealer, Pepper, Obadiah | |
| Catalyst (12) | Ambush. Hit by his own company's weapon, he's left with shrapnel in his heart | Ambush (victim, not fighter) |
| Debate (12–25) | Captivity in the cave. Build the missile or die? | |
| Break into Two (25) | Escape in the Mark I. Yinsen dies | **Fight #1: crude, costly, barely works** |
| B Story (30) | Pepper, who represents the human heart he's neglecting | |
| Fun & Games (30–55) | Building the Mark II, learning to fly, the comic failures | Training (rules established) |
| Midpoint (55) | Learns his weapons are arming the Ten Rings and flies to Gulmira | **Fight #2: the "promise of the premise" power fantasy** |
| Bad Guys Close In (55–75) | Obadiah's betrayal is revealed. He steals the arc reactor | |
| All Is Lost (75) | Tony is paralyzed and dying, his heart literally ripped out | |
| Dark Night (75–85) | Crawls to the old reactor, "Proof that Tony Stark has a heart" | |
| Break into Three (85) | Suits up in a damaged suit, outmatched | |
| Finale (85–110) | Iron Monger fight. Tony wins with brains (icing, the reactor overload), not strength | **Fight #3: hero outgunned, wins by sacrifice and wit** |
| Final Image (110) | "I am Iron Man." A changed man, owning the consequences | |

**Lesson:** three main fights, placed at Break into Two, Midpoint and Finale. That pattern is a good template for an indie budget.

### 2c. The hero's journey: *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (2018)

| Stage | Miles Morales | Fight beat |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary World | Brooklyn, a new elite school, pressure from his dad | |
| Call to Adventure | Spider bite. Witnesses Spider-Man die at the collider | Collider battle (watched, not fought) |
| Refusal | Can't control his powers and fears the responsibility | |
| Mentor | Peter B. Parker, burned out and reluctant | |
| Crossing the Threshold | Breaking into Alchemax | Chase with Doc Ock (comic, chaotic) |
| Tests, Allies, Enemies | The Spider-Gang. Learning the rules | Aunt May's house brawl (team vs. villains) |
| Ordeal | Uncle Aaron is the Prowler and dies protecting Miles | Prowler chase. **A fight he can't win because of who the enemy is** |
| Reward | Dad's speech through the door, the "Leap of Faith" | |
| Resurrection | Final collider battle vs. Kingpin | **Climax: uses his unique powers, venom and invisibility** |
| Return with Elixir | Brooklyn's own Spider-Man | |

**Lesson:** the hero's journey is the clearest shape for an *origin*. The fights test growth, not just strength.

### 2d. Fights as thematic escalation in other key films

- **The Dark Knight (2008):** the fights get *less* effective as the film goes on. The interrogation room is a "fight" Batman loses ("You have nothing to threaten me with"). The climax is the ferry experiment plus a short skirmish in the Prewitt Building: a philosophical victory, not a physical one.
- **Logan (2017):** every fight costs Logan more because his healing is failing. The final villain, X-24, is literally his younger, rage-filled self. The mirror becomes literal.
- **Unbreakable (2000):** a $75M studio film ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbreakable_(film))) with essentially *one* fight, the home invasion rescue in the third act. It works because 90 minutes built up the question "is he real?"
- **Chronicle (2012):** small incidents escalate into a city-wide fight between cousins. The hero/villain split happens *inside* the friend group.

## 3. Designing the Hero

### Want vs. need
- **Want:** conscious and external (*get the girl, avenge Dad, make rent*).
- **Need:** unconscious and internal (*accept responsibility, let people in, forgive himself*).
- **The climax should force a choice between them.** Peter gives up MJ (want) for responsibility (need).

### Flaw and wound
- **Wound:** the past event that hurts (Ben's death, Bruce's parents, Logan's decades of killing).
- **Flaw:** the behavior the wound produces (selfishness, control, isolation).
- **Rule:** the flaw should *lose* the hero a fight early on, and overcoming it should *win* the final one.

### Power set: rules and costs
Brandon Sanderson's laws of magic apply directly to superpowers. **First Law:** the ability to solve conflict with a power is "directly proportional to how well the reader understands" it. **Second Law:** "Limitations > Powers." He argues Superman is interesting because of his ethics and kryptonite, not his strength ([Sanderson, Second Law](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/sandersons-second-law/); [Coppermind summary](https://coppermind.net/wiki/Sanderson%27s_Laws_of_Magic)).

**Why limits make fights work:**
1. **Suspense needs doubt.** If the hero can't lose, the fight is only spectacle.
2. **Costs force choices.** Each use of the power spends something: blood, memory, time or a relationship.
3. **Clear rules allow clever wins.** If the audience knows the rules, a final victory that bends them feels earned, not like cheating.
4. **Limits are where originality comes from.** Sanderson notes it's "virtually impossible" to invent a new power, but a new *limitation* is fresh.

**Cost ideas:** physical (nosebleeds, aging, hunger), range or duration limits, a recharge time, collateral damage, emotional triggers (Fast Color's powers cause earthquakes), social costs (exposure, being hunted, as in Code 8).

### Origin handling: don't spend 60 minutes on it
- **Start late:** open with powers already active (Logan, Hancock), or show the origin in a 3-minute cold open.
- **Make it Act 1 only:** get the powers by page 10–15, and finish testing them by the midpoint at the latest.
- **Make the origin the mystery:** Unbreakable and Chronicle turn *discovery* into the plot itself.
- **Assume familiarity:** Spider-Verse jokes "you know the story." Audiences know the shape of an origin, so skip the parts they can fill in.

### Secret identity questions (answer them all on paper)
- Who knows, and when do they find out?
- What does exposure cost? (A job, family safety, arrest?)
- Does the mask *protect* anyone, or is it vanity?
- Is there an identity at all? Iron Man's public reveal *was* the ending.
- **Fight lever:** the villain learning the identity is a powerful midpoint or Act 3 escalation, as when the Goblin learns Peter's.

## 4. Designing the Villain

### The villain is a mirror
The best villains share the hero's wound but choose the opposite response. Write the villain's backstory *first*, because often the villain drives the plot more than the hero does.

| Villain | Mirror of hero | Understandable goal | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Killmonger** (*Black Panther*) | Both are sons of murdered fathers, heirs to Wakanda | Arm the oppressed with Wakanda's power | His philosophy is convincing, and he's "the hero of his own story." T'Challa ends up adopting part of his goal ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/movies/2018/2/16/17017938/erik-killmonger-black-panther-best-marvel-villain)) |
| **Vulture** (*Spider-Man: Homecoming*) | Both are working-class New Yorkers. Peter wants into the elite that Toomes resents | Provide for his family after the elites took his salvage job | Director Jon Watts wanted villains "with problems that people can relate to" ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/33958-vulture-in-spider-man-homecoming-is-marvels-best-villain)). The car-ride reveal makes the threat personal *without* a fight |
| **The Joker** (*The Dark Knight*) | Both are masked vigilantes outside the law. "You complete me." | To prove anyone can be corrupted | He attacks Batman's *ideals*, not his body, so physical victories don't count |
| **Thanos** (*Infinity War*) | Sacrifices for a cause, the way heroes do | Prevent extinction through "balance" | He pays a real cost (Gamora) and *wins*. A clear goal plus sacrifice makes a villain a protagonist |

**Villain checklist:** a clear goal that's reachable without the hero, a reason that a sane person could agree with 30% of, a method the hero must reject, a power or advantage that targets the hero's *specific* weakness, and at least one scene where they win.

## 5. Theme: Linking Hero, Villain and Fights

- **Theme is an argument, not a topic.** "Power" is a topic. "Power without restraint destroys the one who holds it" (Chronicle) is a theme.
- **The hero and villain embody opposite answers** to the same thematic question.
- **Each fight should test the theme**, and the hero's approach should change between fights:

| Fight | Thematic function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **First fight** | Hero fights with the *flaw* and wins badly or loses | Peter lets the thief go |
| **Midpoint fight** | Hero wins with the "false" approach. The victory costs something | Tony at Gulmira: violence solves it, for now |
| **Low-point fight** | Hero's approach collapses. The villain's argument looks right | Batman in the interrogation room |
| **Climax** | Hero wins by *embodying the theme*, often by not doing what the villain expects | T'Challa turns off the suits. Peter chooses responsibility |

**Test for every fight:** if you could swap this fight with another fight in the movie without changing the story, it's not doing thematic work. Rewrite the stakes.

## 6. Originality: Clichés to Avoid or Subvert

| Cliché | Fresh angle |
|---|---|
| Sky beam / portal climax | A contained climax with personal stakes (Logan's forest, Unbreakable's house) |
| Villain is "hero but evil" with the same powers | Asymmetric powers, so the hero must out-think rather than out-punch |
| 60-minute origin story | Start with the powers active, or make the origin the mystery |
| Dead parent as the only motivation | A living, complicating relationship (Psychokinesis's estranged father and daughter) |
| Love interest as a hostage | The love interest has her own goal that conflicts with the hero's |
| City-destroying stakes | Stakes measured in one family, one block, one relationship |

**How to make an original IP feel fresh:**
- **Genre mash-up:** superhero plus another genre. Brightburn is "Superman as a horror film," Code 8 is a heist and crime story, Fast Color is a family drama.
- **A specific place and culture:** Psychokinesis is a story about Seoul redevelopment evictions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychokinesis_(film))). Specific beats generic.
- **A new limitation, not a new power** (see §3).
- **Ask the question the big studios avoid:** class, the cost of policing, aging, disability, what happens to the people the hero saves.

### Legal risk: don't drift too close to Marvel or DC
- **Ideas aren't protected. Specific expression is.** "A strong man who flies" is free to use. A red-caped, S-shielded alien journalist isn't.
- **This has happened before:** DC sued Fawcett over Captain Marvel's similarity to Superman (capes, strength, speed, flight). The appeals court found infringement, and Fawcett settled for $400,000 and stopped publishing the character. DC had already won a similar case over "Wonder Man" ([UC Law Review](https://uclawreview.org/2019/03/07/will-the-real-captain-marvel-please-stand-up/); [Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2019/05/29/copyright-trademark-and-the-tale-of-captain-marvel/)).
- **"Super Hero" as a word:** the USPTO cancelled four jointly owned Marvel/DC "Super Hero" registrations in September 2024 by default, after they didn't respond. They still hold a 2018 "SUPER HEROES" and a 1985 "SUPER-VILLAIN" registration ([Womble Bond Dickinson](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/us/insights/alerts/its-bird-its-plane-its-public-domain-uspto-cancels-marvel-and-dc-comics-trademarks)). Avoid those words in your **title and marketing**, and get a clearance check.
- **Practical safeguards:** no lookalike costumes, logos, names or color schemes. Keep dated drafts as evidence of independent creation. Get a title and script clearance report before you sell or distribute (E&O insurers will ask for one). Run anything that feels close past an entertainment lawyer.

## 7. Writing on a Budget

| Film | Budget | Producibility trick |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronicle** (2012) | ~$12M, grossed $125M+ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicle_(film))) | Found footage hides VFX limits. Three teenage leads. Shot in Cape Town to save money |
| **Brightburn** (2019) | $6–12M, grossed $32.9M ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Brightburn)) | Horror grammar: powers shown through *aftermath* and suggestion. Rural farmhouse locations |
| **Fast Color** (2018) | Low budget, outside the studio system ([Comics Beat](https://www.comicsbeat.com/fast-color-review/)) | Powers used sparingly and shown as practical color and particle effects. A three-woman family drama |
| **Psychokinesis** (2018) | Mid-budget Korean film, Netflix distribution | Comedy lowers the VFX bar. One neighborhood, one cause |
| **Code 8** (2019) | Proof-of-concept short, then $1.7M+ crowdfunded on Indiegogo, then a ~$15M feature ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/?p=83447)) | A short proved the world existed. Powers are illegal, so they're hidden and rare on screen |
| **Unbreakable** (2000) | $75M (studio), grossed $248M ([The Numbers](https://www.the-numbers.com/movie/Unbreakable)) | Proves how far *restraint* can go: one fight, maximum dread |

**Budget-writing rules:**
1. **Write for 8–12 main locations.** Every new location means another company move.
2. **Choose powers that are cheap to show:** strength, durability, speed implied by editing, telekinesis on small objects, mind powers, healing and invisibility (an empty frame is free). Flight, transformation, energy blasts and giant creatures are expensive.
3. **Show the result, not the process.** A dented car, a frightened witness, a nosebleed.
4. **Limit set pieces to 3–4 major fights** (Break into Two, Midpoint, low point, Climax). Make the rest tension, chases and near-misses.
5. **Make the power's *limits* the story,** since restraint is cheaper than spectacle.
6. **Character-driven stakes:** a small cast of 3–6 principals. The climax can be one room if the relationship is at stake.
7. **Ground the fights in martial arts and stunts, with VFX enhancing them,** not the other way around.
8. **Consider a 10–15 minute proof-of-concept short** of one key fight (the Code 8 route).

## 8. Templates

### 8a. Logline formula
> When **[INCITING INCIDENT]** gives **[FLAWED HERO, 2–4 words]** **[POWER + ITS COST]**, they must **[CONCRETE GOAL]** before **[VILLAIN]** **[VILLAIN'S PLAN]**, or else **[PERSONAL STAKES]**.

*Example:* When a lab accident lets a guilt-ridden paramedic heal others by absorbing their injuries, she must stop a grieving surgeon who's harvesting powered people to resurrect his son, or else lose the sister he's already taken.

**Check:** Is the flaw clear? Is the cost clear? Could the stakes fit in one house?

### 8b. Beginning / Middle / End expander (3 sentences to 15 beats)
Write your 3 sentences first:
- **BEGINNING:** ______________________
- **MIDDLE:** ______________________
- **END:** ______________________

Then expand them:

| # | Beat | Your story (1–2 lines) | Fight? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BEGINNING** | | | |
| 1 | Opening Image: hero's flawed normal life | | |
| 2 | Theme Stated: someone voices the question | | |
| 3 | Wound & Want: what they chase and why | | |
| 4 | Catalyst: powers arrive / the villain strikes | | ☐ |
| 5 | Debate: rules discovered, cost first felt | | |
| 6 | Break into Two: hero chooses to act | | ☐ **Fight 1 (flaw shows)** |
| **MIDDLE** | | | |
| 7 | B Story: the relationship that carries the theme | | |
| 8 | Fun & Games: power fantasy, rules tested | | ☐ |
| 9 | Midpoint: false victory OR identity exposed | | ☐ **Fight 2 (wins, at a cost)** |
| 10 | Villain Escalates: targets the hero's personal life | | |

### 8c. Character sheet (fill one in for the hero AND one for the villain)
```
NAME / ALIAS:

```

```

WHAT IT DOES (1 sentence):

VISUAL SIGNATURE (cheap to shoot?):  Practical / Light VFX / Heavy VFX

```

## 9. Tools

### Screenwriting software
Prices vary by source and date, so check the vendor before buying ([Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/best-screenwriting-software-78776/); [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/screenwriting-software/)).

| Tool | Price (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Final Draft 13** | ~$249 one-time, 30-day trial | The industry standard. Best if you're handing scripts to production staff |
| **WriterSolo** | Pay-what-you-want from $0 at launch ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/writersolo-screenwriting-software)) | Desktop app from the WriterDuet team. Check current pricing |
| **Highland 2** | Free, Pro upgrade paid | Mac only. Plain-text Fountain format, good for drafting |
| **Fade In** | ~$80 one-time | Pro features, cross-platform, the best value |
| **Celtx** | Subscription with a limited free tier | Cloud-based, with links to scheduling and budgeting |

### Formatting basics
- 12-pt Courier, 1.0–1.5" left margin, ~1" right, top and bottom.
- **Scene heading:** `INT./EXT. LOCATION - DAY/NIGHT`.
- **Action:** present tense, visual, no more than 3–4 lines per block.
- **Character name** in CAPS on first appearance. Dialogue is centered under the character cue.
- **Fight scenes:** write the *story* of the fight (who has the upper hand, what changes, what it costs) in short punchy lines. Don't write every punch; the stunt coordinator will choreograph them.

### The page-per-minute rule
One page is roughly one minute of screen time. That's an average, not a law: Stephen Follows's study of about 2,500 scripts found a median of 119 pages for a 108-minute film, or about 55 seconds per page ([Stephen Follows](https://stephenfollows.com/is-the-page-per-minute-rule-correct/)). Dialogue pages play faster, while action pages often play slower ([StudioBinder](https://studiobinder.com/blog/how-long-is-a-movie-script)). **A target of 95–110 pages suits an indie.** Remember that a half-page fight description can easily take 3 minutes and several shoot days.

### Where to get feedback
1. **Writing groups:** free and ongoing. Try local film groups, Facebook/Reddit (r/Screenwriting), Stage 32 or InkTip communities. Trade reads one-for-one.
2. **Table read:** have actors (even friends) read the draft aloud. It's the fastest way to find dead dialogue.
3. **Paid coverage:** a professional reader writes a 3–10 page report. Services like WeScreenplay, Coverfly-linked readers and ScreenCraft typically charge ~$60–$150+. Use it on Draft 3+, not Draft 1.
4. **The Black List (blcklst.com):** host a script for ~$30/month and buy evaluations (~$75–$100). High scores are promoted to industry members, and a fee-waiver program exists ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/how-does-the-black-list-work); [Filmustage](https://filmustage.com/blog/best-script-coverage-services-in-2025-what-professional-readers-wont-tell-you/)). Only submit a polished draft. Contests (Austin Film Festival, Nicholl, ScreenCraft) are the other route to credibility.

## Quick Start: Your Next 30 Days
1. **Week 1:** Write 20 loglines and pick one. Fill in both character sheets.
2. **Week 2:** Fill in the power-rules sheet. Write the one-page synopsis, *ending included*.
3. **Week 3:** Fill in the 15-beat expander and mark exactly 3–4 fights.
4. **Week 4:** For each fight, write one sentence each on what the hero wants, what they could lose, what the power can't do and how the fight tests the theme. If you can't answer all four, rework the beat before you draft.
